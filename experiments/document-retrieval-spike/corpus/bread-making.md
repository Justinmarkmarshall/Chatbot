# Workshop bread session

The fictional Cedar kitchen runs a beginner bread session using a simple wheat dough. This document records the session's own recipe and observation notes. It is not a universal formula for every flour or loaf. Participants keep mixing, fermentation, final proof, baking, and cooling as distinct stages. Several stages involve waiting, but the reason for waiting changes as the dough becomes a baked loaf.

## Mixing and hydration

The session recipe combines flour, water, yeast, and salt. Water is measured against flour weight so the group can repeat the same dough consistency. Add most of the planned water first and mix until no dry flour remains. The instructor then checks the dough before deciding whether to add the reserved water. Different flour batches can absorb water differently, so the worksheet records what was actually added.

Mixing distributes ingredients and begins developing the dough structure. A sticky surface immediately after mixing does not necessarily mean the recipe failed. Allow the scheduled rest before making changes, then compare the dough with the instructor's sample. Adding large amounts of flour at every sticky moment can change the recipe enough to make later comparisons meaningless.

## Bulk fermentation

Bulk fermentation is the first main rise before the dough is divided and shaped. Yeast activity produces gas while the dough changes in texture and volume. The session records room temperature and dough appearance instead of relying only on the clock. A cooler room can lengthen the time needed to reach a similar stage, so two bowls started together may not become ready at exactly the same minute.

The instructor demonstrates gentle folds during the early part of this stage. These folds are not the same as final shaping. They help organize the dough while fermentation continues. Participants note whether the dough holds its shape more readily after each fold, but they avoid repeatedly pressing out all the gas merely to measure progress.

## Shaping and final proof

After bulk fermentation, shape the dough and let the shaped loaf complete its final proof before baking. Final proof occurs after shaping, whereas bulk fermentation occurs before shaping. The distinction matters when comparing a loaf that spread during shaping with one that collapsed just before baking. Both can look disappointing, but they describe different points in the process and should have separate notes.

The workshop uses a gentle indentation test alongside visual expansion to discuss readiness. It does not present a single minute count as correct for every room temperature. Participants compare the shaped loaf with the earlier photographs, recording whether the surface looks tense, relaxed, or fragile. The observation sheet keeps those descriptions separate from the oven settings used in the next stage.

## Baking and crust

Preheat the oven before loading the loaf so the initial baking environment is consistent across the session. The instructor manages the hot trays and any planned steam. Oven temperature, loading time, and loaf position are recorded because these can influence browning. A pale crust and a dense interior are not automatically the same problem; the group inspects both before proposing an explanation.

The workshop compares loaves only after recording their approximate size and shape. A small roll can bake differently from a large round loaf under the same setting. Participants should not assume that copying the final crust colour from a photograph proves that the interior has reached the same condition.

## Cooling before slicing

Leave the baked loaf on a rack until it has cooled before slicing it. Cutting a very hot loaf can leave the interior looking gummy because the crumb has not finished setting and moisture is still redistributing. This cooling wait is different from proofing: the loaf has already been baked, and the purpose is no longer to let the dough rise.

The tasting worksheet closes with these observations:

- Compare crust colour and thickness.
- Note the crumb texture after cooling.
- Record mixing and fermentation changes separately.
- Keep one clear explanation for each proposed next-session adjustment.
