# Workshop tomato bed

The fictional Cedar garden maintains a small outdoor tomato bed as a teaching project. These notes describe that project's routines rather than a treatment prescription for every climate or plant disease. Volunteers observe leaves, stems, soil, and fruit before changing the routine. A wilted leaf is a symptom with several possible causes, so the shift note includes recent weather and watering instead of immediately naming a disease.

## Preparing seedlings for outdoor planting

Harden off indoor-grown seedlings by gradually exposing them to outdoor conditions before permanent planting. The workshop begins with short sheltered periods and increases exposure over several days. This transition is different from watering a mature plant or removing side shoots. Young seedlings can be stressed by sudden changes in sun, wind, and temperature even when their potting mix contains enough moisture.

Before moving a seedling into the bed, check its label and the planned support position. The workshop records planting dates so later volunteers can distinguish recently transplanted plants from established ones. A temporary pause in growth after transplanting should be interpreted alongside those dates rather than compared directly with an older plant that has occupied its position for weeks.

## Watering and moisture consistency

Check the soil before watering rather than relying only on whether the leaf surface looks tired in afternoon heat. Apply water near the root zone and keep the watering record consistent between shifts. The workshop aims for steady moisture instead of alternating severe drying with a large late soak. A volunteer who sees a wet surface should still consider the moisture below it and the amount applied on the previous shift.

Uneven moisture can contribute to fruit splitting as developing fruit experiences changing water availability. The teaching bed compares watering notes with the timing of observed splits. This is a different observation from a chewed hole or a damaged stem. Do not combine every damaged fruit under one label, because that makes the record less useful when the group reviews possible causes.

## Supports and side shoots

The garden uses labelled supports so heavy plants do not fall across the walking path. Tie stems gently and leave room for growth rather than fastening them tightly against a rigid edge. The volunteer checks old ties during each inspection because a comfortable tie can become restrictive as the stem thickens. Supporting a stem is different from pruning it, and either action should be recorded separately.

Side-shoot management depends on the plant type and the teaching plan. The workshop marks which plants are being trained to a selected form. Volunteers do not remove every shoot from every tomato plant simply because a neighbouring plant was pruned. If the label is missing, ask the garden lead before changing the plant's structure.

## Pollination and fruit set

Flowers appearing on a plant do not guarantee that every flower will develop into a mature fruit. Temperature and growing conditions influence the outcome. The workshop records flowering dates and later fruit set as separate observations. This helps the group distinguish a plant that has not flowered from one that flowered but formed few fruits, rather than treating both as the same failure to grow.

The teaching notes also separate fruit set from ripening. A small green fruit has already formed even though it is not ready for harvest. Adding more water does not transform that distinction into a watering diagnosis. Keep the plant's stage in the record before deciding which part of the routine needs closer attention.

## Harvest and inspection notes

Inspect fruit for maturity and visible damage during the planned garden round. Handle fruit gently and keep damaged examples separate for discussion. The fictional project does not use this document to identify unknown diseases or recommend chemical treatments. If a new symptom spreads, photograph it with the plant label and refer it to the garden lead.

- Record the plant identifier and date.
- Separate leaf, stem, and fruit observations.
- Include recent watering and weather.
- Check the training label before pruning.

A consistent record is more useful than several conflicting changes made by different volunteers during the same week.
